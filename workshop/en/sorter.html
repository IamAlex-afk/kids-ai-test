Why did the sorter get it wrong?

Difficulty:

  1. 5–7, with an adult
  2. 8–10
  3. 11–13
  4. 14+

Goal

Find what made the sorter wrong and fix it with examples. Compare "before" and "after".

Explanation

This is a teaching model, not a neural network. It does not "see" the picture: every card already comes with two ready-made features — shape and background. The model tries a rule by shape and a rule by background and keeps the one that makes fewer mistakes on the training cards.

Wrong! It is a circle. In training, every circle was on a blue background and every square on a yellow one. So the rule "yellow background → square" fitted the training cards just as well as the rule by shape.

When two rules fit equally well, our sorter picks the background — that is how this demo is set up, so that the mistake becomes visible.

It worked: the training cards now have circles and squares on different backgrounds. The background rule now makes mistakes, the shape rule does not.

Nothing changed: the cards you added repeat the old pattern (circles on blue, squares on yellow). More of the same does not help — you need examples that break the pattern. Go back and pick others.

The sorter was wrong not because it is "stupid", but because the training examples had a false hint in them — the background.

For the adult

Goal

The child sees that a model learns from examples and can latch onto an accidental hint (the background), and that this is fixed with examples that contradict the hint.

Questions to talk about

  • Why did the sorter call the circle a square?
  • Which cards helped and which did not? Why?
  • Where in real life could a "trap hint" like this appear?

Common misconception

More data is always better. Not so: ten identical examples teach nothing new; what matters is that examples differ where it matters.

Signs of understanding

  • Explains the mistake as "in the examples all the circles were on blue".
  • Picks a card that breaks the false pattern — and does it again in the new example.
  • Does not assume the model "sees" the picture the way a person does.

How the activity works

For the adult: the model is a one-feature rule. At first both features fit the training cards equally well (0 mistakes); on a tie this demo picks the background — that is our rule, not a property of machine learning. The model receives ready-made features, not pixels.

Printable task

Draw 4 cards: 2 circles on blue and 2 squares on yellow. Cover the shapes and ask: what else could someone "guess" by? Add one card that breaks the "background" hint.

Answers and explanation (for the adult)

The trap hint is the background colour: in the examples it always matches the shape. A circle on yellow or a square on blue breaks it.

Sources

  1. Geirhos et al., "Shortcut learning in deep neural networks", Nature Machine Intelligence, 2020
    What it supports: Machine-learning models can rely on an accidental cue in the training data (such as the background) instead of the intended feature.
  2. Holte, "Very Simple Classification Rules Perform Well on Most Commonly Used Datasets", Machine Learning, 1993
    What it supports: A one-feature rule chosen by the fewest mistakes is a known simple classification method; our sorter is a teaching version of it.
