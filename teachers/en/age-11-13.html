AI lesson for grades 6–8: AI guesses the next word

Grades 6–8 (ages 11–13) · 45 min

Lesson goal

Students understand that a chatbot builds an answer by picking a likely next word rather than looking up the truth, and can check an answer against a second source.

AI4K12 big ideas: representation and reasoning; natural interaction. UNESCO: ethics of AI and AI techniques, "understand" and "apply" levels (our estimate).

What you need

  • A screen.
  • A "Machine library" for each group — 8 sentences: "Paris is the capital of France." "Berlin is the capital of Germany." "Rome is the capital of Italy." "Madrid is the capital of Spain." "Sydney is the largest city in Australia." "Sydney has a famous opera house." "Sydney is a city in Australia." "Tourists in Australia visit Sydney."
  • An atlas or geography book for fact-checking.

Lesson on this site: "AI doesn't know the truth — it guesses the next word" →

Lesson flow (45 minutes)

  1. 5 min. Warm-up. Write "I'm going to the…" on the board. The class suggests the next word, like a phone keyboard. Which word came up most, and why?
  2. 7 min. Site lesson. Read it and answer "Check yourself".
  3. 13 min. The Human Chatbot. Groups of 4 may use only the "Machine library". Task: finish "The capital of Australia is…". The answer is the city that appears most often in the same sentence as "Australia". In the library that is Sydney (3 sentences out of 3), and the word "Canberra" is not there at all — the machine cannot pick it.
  4. 8 min. Fact-check. Open the atlas: the capital of Australia is Canberra. Was the machine "lying"? No: it does not know what is true, it picks a frequent word. Did it sound confident? Yes. Tell the class: real models are more complex and compute probabilities over huge amounts of text, but the principle is the same — they pick what is likely, not what is checked.
  5. 8 min. Discussion — see the questions below.
  6. 4 min. Wrap-up. Each student writes their own one-sentence rule for checking an AI answer.

Discussion questions

  • Why doesn't a chatbot say "I don't know" instead of answering confidently?
  • What is the difference between "appears often" and "is true"?
  • How can you check an AI answer? Where do you find a second source?
  • When is an AI mistake dangerous, and when must you ask an adult? (health, money, safety)

How to check understanding

A student explains in their own words why the group wrote "Sydney" and names two ways to check an answer.

Differentiation

  • For students who need support: a library of 4 sentences instead of 8.
  • Early finishers write their own "library" that makes the machine wrong about a different question.

Note for families

Today we looked at why chatbots sometimes state wrong things confidently: they pick a likely word rather than look up a fact. At home, ask your child to show you how to check an AI answer against a second source.

Sources
