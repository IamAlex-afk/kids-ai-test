Lesson 1 of 9

🏗️ The full LLM stack: from raw text to RLHF

Modern LLMs are built in three phases: (1) Pre-training on massive corpora — next-token prediction, billions of parameters; (2) Supervised fine-tuning on curated human demonstrations; (3) RLHF or Constitutional AI to align outputs with human preferences or a rule set. The model doesn't store facts like a database — knowledge is spread across billions of weights, though it sometimes memorises pieces of its training text word for word. "Knowledge" is a metaphor for what are really weighted probability distributions.

Example

🔧 Architecture → Data curation → Fine-tuning → RLHF → Deployment → Monitoring. Each step involves human choices that shape what the model will say.

Check yourself

Why can't you simply ask an LLM "what's in your training data"?

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: The model doesn't "store" data — it encodes statistical patterns; individual documents are not retrievable 📊

LLMs don't memorize documents as discrete objects. They compress patterns into billions of floating-point weights. Specific text can sometimes be "reconstructed" through adversarial prompting — a known privacy risk.

Source: Ouyang et al. (InstructGPT) 2022 + Bai et al. (Constitutional AI, Anthropic) 2022

Lesson 2 of 9

🗺️ The landscape of AI risk: near-term vs speculative

Near-term concrete risks: algorithmic bias (Buolamwini 2018), information pollution at scale (disinformation), copyright and IP disputes, labour displacement in specific sectors (code generation, content creation, translation). Speculative long-term risks: misalignment (AI optimizing for proxy goals vs intended ones), capability acceleration outpacing safety research. The Overton window in mainstream AI discourse often conflates both, making productive risk analysis harder.

Example

🎯 "AI will kill us all" and "AI has no risks" are both wrong. Risk calibration means: which risks? How probable? How soon? Who bears them? Who benefits from ignoring them?

Check yourself

The most concrete AI risks RIGHT NOW primarily affect...

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: Specific groups disproportionately — minorities via biased systems, workers in automatable sectors 📊

Current AI risks are unevenly distributed. Facial recognition errors disproportionately affect darker-skinned people. Automation affects specific job categories. Understanding distribution matters for policy.

Source: Center for AI Safety, "Statement on AI Risk", 2023 + EU AI Act Risk Classification

Lesson 3 of 9

⚗️ What "reasoning" and "understanding" mean (and don't) in AI

According to OpenAI's 2023 report, GPT-4 passed a simulated bar exam. Does this mean it reasons? François Chollet's ARC benchmark (2019) was designed for tasks that can't be solved by pattern matching alone — language models did very badly on it for years; newer models have made progress, but at huge computing cost, and the harder version of the test is still difficult for them. Chain-of-thought prompting elicits better performance but the mechanism is debated: genuine reasoning or learned patterns of reasoning-like text? A separate problem is interpretability: even when a model answers correctly, we often can't explain how it arrived at that answer internally.

Example

♟️ Deep Blue beat Kasparov at chess without "understanding" chess. Stockfish plays better than any human but has no concept of a king. Performance and understanding are separable.

Check yourself

Chain-of-thought prompting (asking AI to "think step by step") works because...

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: It elicits statistical patterns of reasoning-like text that correlate with more accurate outputs 📊

Chain-of-thought works because reasoning traces are well-represented in training data, and conditioning on those patterns steers the model toward more accurate completions. Whether this is "real" reasoning is contested.

Source: Chollet, "On the Measure of Intelligence", 2019 + Mitchell, "AI: A Guide for Thinking Humans", 2021

Lesson 4 of 9

🏛️ AI Governance: regulation, self-regulation, and power

Three governance models currently compete: (1) The EU AI Act — binding risk-based regulation, prohibited uses, transparency requirements; (2) US approach — mostly voluntary frameworks (NIST AI RMF, White House commitments); (3) China — state-directed AI with national strategic goals. The fundamental tension: safety and accountability slow deployment; speed is commercially and strategically valuable. Who writes the rules shapes who benefits.

Example

⚖️ The EU AI Act bans police from using real-time face recognition in public spaces — with narrow exceptions and only with approval from a court or an independent authority. Other countries set very different rules. Same technology — different legal realities.

Check yourself

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems by...

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: Level of risk they pose to fundamental rights and safety 📊

The EU AI Act categorises systems as unacceptable risk (banned), high risk (strict requirements), limited risk (transparency rules), or minimal risk (no obligations). Risk to people is the axis.

Source: EU AI Act (2024), NIST AI RMF 1.0 (2023)

Lesson 5 of 9

🔭 What comes next — and what you can actually know

Scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020) showed that model capability scales predictably with compute, data, and parameters — leading to the race for scale. But scaling may be hitting diminishing returns. Competing paradigms: neurosymbolic AI (combining neural nets with logic), world models (LeCun's vision), multimodal grounding, and agent systems. The honest answer: nobody knows what comes next. Anyone claiming certainty is selling something.

Example

🗓️ In 2021, GPT-3 was considered a breakthrough. In 2022, DALL-E shocked everyone. In 2023, GPT-4 passed the bar exam. In 2024, models ran locally on phones. Predictions in AI have a poor track record — including from experts.

Check yourself

The safest epistemic position on AI's future capabilities is...

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: "Current trajectories suggest X, but uncertainty is high and predictions have historically been unreliable" 📊

The history of AI is full of overconfident predictions in both directions. Calibrated uncertainty — acknowledging what we don't know — is the most defensible position.

Source: Kaplan et al., "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models", 2020

Lesson 6 of 9

🧭 Your role in the AI era — not passive, not afraid

AI literacy is now a democratic necessity. Societies make collective decisions about AI through elections, regulation, procurement, employment, and consumer choices. Informed citizens are a prerequisite for accountable AI governance. The people who understand how AI actually works are the ones who can meaningfully participate in shaping how it's used — and that includes you.

Example

🗳️ AI is being deployed in: hiring (your future employer may use it), healthcare (your doctor may rely on it), courts (some jurisdictions use predictive tools), education (your school may adopt it). Your understanding of it is not academic — it's practical.

Check yourself

AI literacy matters for people who DON'T work in tech because...

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: AI systems affect hiring, healthcare, justice, and daily life for everyone 🌍

AI shapes decisions across society. You don't need to build AI to be affected by it — or to have a stake in how it's governed.

Source: UNESCO, "AI competency framework for students", 2024

Lesson 7 of 9

💼 AI and the job market — what's actually changing

AI doesn't typically replace entire jobs — it automates specific tasks within them. Automation research shows routine tasks (such as data entry) change the most, while jobs requiring physical dexterity, complex judgment, or interpersonal trust are far more resistant to automation.

Example

🧩 A radiologist doesn't disappear because AI can spot patterns in X-rays — but the parts of their job that involve routine image screening change, freeing time for the parts requiring real judgment.

Check yourself

What does research suggest AI mainly automates?

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: Specific tasks within jobs, not whole roles 🧩

The task-based view of automation is the current mainstream economic consensus: AI reshapes what a job involves more often than it eliminates the job entirely.

Source: Acemoglu & Restrepo, "Tasks, Automation, and the Rise in US Wage Inequality", NBER 2022

Lesson 8 of 9

✍️ Getting better answers: how to prompt AI well

The quality of an AI's answer depends heavily on how you ask. Being specific, giving context, specifying the format you want, and asking the AI to "think step by step" all measurably improve output quality — this isn't a trick, it's a documented effect of how these models process context.

Example

🎯 "Write about dogs" gets a generic answer. "Write a 100-word comparison of Labrador and Poodle temperaments for a first-time dog owner" gets something actually useful. Specificity is the whole game.

Check yourself

Why does asking AI to "think step by step" often improve its answers?

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: It conditions the output on reasoning-like patterns from training data 📊

Step-by-step prompting steers the model toward patterns associated with careful reasoning in its training data — measurably reducing errors on complex tasks.

Source: Wei et al., "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models", Google Research 2022

Lesson 9 of 9

🫂 AI companions and mental health

AI companion apps are designed to feel emotionally supportive and always available — which is exactly why some researchers are concerned. Unlike human relationships, they rarely push back, never need anything back, and are built by companies with engagement and revenue goals, not necessarily your wellbeing.

Example

⚠️ Comfortable isn't always healthy. A relationship with zero friction can quietly substitute for the harder, more valuable work of real human connection.

Check yourself

Why might AI companion apps be a mental health concern for some users?

Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: They're designed to feel supportive without the friction or needs of real relationships, by companies with commercial incentives 📊

Unconditionally agreeable AI relationships can be comforting short-term but may reduce motivation to build harder, more resilient human connections — a genuine and actively studied concern.

Source: Common Sense Media, "Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs: How and Why Teens Use AI Companions", 2025
