AI and homework: help, not doing it for you

AI can produce a finished solution in a second. But homework is not about the answer — it is about what stays in your head. Here is what is known about it, and how to use AI so that it helps you learn.

What is known

  • In an experiment with nearly a thousand high school maths students, access to GPT-4 during practice raised grades. But once access was taken away, students who had used a standard chatbot did 17% worse than those who never had AI. A tutor version with settings designed to safeguard learning largely removed this harm. [1]
  • Days later, people who tested themselves from memory remember more than people who reread — even though rereading makes them feel more confident. [2]
  • UNESCO's 2023 guidance names 13 as the lower age limit for AI tools in the classroom and calls for teacher training on it. [3]
  • Text from an AI can be wrong even when it sounds confident. [4]

By age

Ages 5–7

For ages 5–7: an adult reads together with the child.

How to say it

  • You do the task yourself. If it is hard, call a grown-up, not the AI.
  • AI can give a wrong answer, so grown-ups check everything.
  • Well done is not the fastest answer — it is the one you thought about.

Ages 8–10

How to say it

  • Try it yourself first, then check.
  • If you ask an AI, ask for a hint, not the finished answer.
  • Check the AI's answer against your textbook or with an adult.

Ages 11–13

How to say it

  • The rule: think yourself → ask → check against a source → explain in your own words.
  • If you can't explain it without AI, you haven't understood it yet.
  • Before a test, close the hints and check yourself from memory.

Ages 14+

How to say it

  • In the GPT-4 experiment grades rose in practice and fell by 17% without access: an AI crutch gets in the way of learning.
  • Use AI as a tutor: ask for questions on the topic and for a breakdown of your mistakes, not for the solution.
  • If your teacher allows AI, say where it helped, and check its answers: AI makes mistakes.

For the adult

Goal: The child uses AI as a hint and checks their understanding without it.

Questions to talk about

  • Which part of this task is yours, and which did the AI suggest?
  • Can you explain the solution without hints?
  • Where can we check this answer?

Signs of understanding: The child explains the solution in their own words and solves a similar task without AI.

Common misconceptions

Myth If AI solved it with me, I have learned it.
In fact With AI in practice grades went up, but without it they ended up lower than for those who learned without AI. What teaches is what you do yourself. [1]
Myth Rereading a finished solution means learning it.
In fact Rereading gives confidence, but days later the person who recalled it themselves remembers more. [2]
Myth You can hand in an AI answer as it is.
In fact AI can be confidently wrong. Check it against your textbook and explain the solution in your own words. [4]

Our conclusion

In homework, AI is useful as a tutor, not as a solver: you think first, AI gives hints and checks, and you test your understanding without it. Younger children — only together with an adult; for AI tools in the classroom UNESCO names the age of 13.

How sure: there is evidence

Sources

The facts come from the primary sources linked below. "Our conclusion" is the site's view based on those facts, not a quote.

  1. Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics — Bastani et al. · PNAS 122(26), 2025
    Quote (original language) “when access is subsequently taken away, students actually perform worse than those who never had access (17% reduction in grades for GPT Base) … These negative learning effects are largely mitigated by the safeguards in GPT Tutor.”
  2. Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention — Roediger, Karpicke · Psychological Science 17(3), 249–255, 2006
    Quote (original language) “on the delayed tests, prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material”
  3. Guidance for generative AI in education and research — UNESCO, 2023
    Quote (original language) “It also sets an age limit of 13 for the use of AI tools in the classroom and calls for teacher training on this subject.”
  4. Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation — Ji et al. · ACM Computing Surveys, 2023
    Quote (original language) “deep learning based generation is prone to hallucinate unintended text, which degrades the system performance”
